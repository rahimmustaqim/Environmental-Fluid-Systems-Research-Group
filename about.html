Indonesia's Renewable Energy Potential: From Fossil Fuel Dependence to a Sustainable Future

A discussion on Indonesia's transition from fossil fuel dependence to a cleaner and more sustainable energy future, highlighting current challenges, renewable energy potential, and the infrastructure needed to support the transition.

  • Energy demand, consumption trends, and the current fossil fuel-dominated energy mix.
  • Opportunities for renewable energy development and the technical, economic, and policy challenges of the energy transition.
  • How Indonesia's archipelagic geography influences energy infrastructure, accessibility, and renewable energy deployment.
Discussion result: One interesting point raised during the discussion was Japan's energy transition experience. In the 1990s, Japan imported liquefied natural gas (LNG) from Indonesia as a cleaner alternative to coal. This suggests that, for Indonesia, a practical transition pathway may first involve shifting from coal to LNG to maintain energy affordability and reliability while reducing emissions. Compared with coal, LNG produces significantly lower CO₂ emissions and emits virtually no sulfur oxides (SOₓ) and much lower nitrogen oxides (NOₓ). Japan's energy mix in 2022 consisted of approximately 22% renewable energy, 6% nuclear, 40% LNG-fired thermal power, and 30% coal. Looking ahead to 2040, Japan aims to increase the share of renewable energy to 40–50%, expand nuclear power to around 20%, and reduce LNG to 30–40%, while further decreasing coal use. This gradual transition demonstrates that cleaner fossil fuels can serve as an effective bridge toward a low-carbon energy system before renewable energy becomes the dominant source. For Indonesia, this transitional approach could provide a realistic pathway while accelerating renewable energy deployment. Although fossil fuels still account for around 82% of Indonesia's electricity generation, the country possesses abundant renewable energy resources—including solar, hydropower, wind, geothermal, and ocean energy—that are more than sufficient to meet future electricity demand. The key challenge, however, lies not in resource availability but in Indonesia's unique archipelagic geography. Renewable energy resources are often located far from major demand centers, making electricity transmission, grid interconnection, and infrastructure development technically complex and economically challenging. Therefore, achieving a successful energy transition will require not only expanding renewable energy capacity but also investing in modern transmission networks, energy storage systems, and integrated grid infrastructure. In this context, LNG could serve as a transitional energy source, helping Indonesia maintain a reliable and affordable electricity supply while gradually increasing the share of renewable energy in its power system.
